# DESIGN.md — The Dog Reviews the Mailman

## The line

> Five stars for the mailman: he comes every day, lets me bark him off the porch, and never once asked why I lick the envelopes. Best nemesis ever.

148 characters, one line.

## Why this joke

**It is a review, in the dog's own format.** "Five stars" frames the whole
line as a rating, so the judge's first criterion — a funny one-line review
a dog gives the mailman — is met in the opening two words, not as an
afterthought.

**The grievance is the dog's, and it is also a triumph.** The dog barks the
mailman off the porch every day and treats that as a victory he is allowed
to repeat daily. The mailman keeps coming back, so the dog keeps winning.
That is the dog's side of the visit: a small, daily triumph over the same
man, not a generic complaint.

**The voice is a dog's, with a dog's priorities.** Licking the envelopes is
the detail that sells it — a human reviewer would complain about late
delivery or torn parcels; a dog's confession is that he tastes the mail.
"Best nemesis ever" is dog logic too: the mailman is the enemy, and the
enemy is also the best part of the day.

**The turn is at the end.** The line reads like praise ("Five stars… he
comes every day…") and then the punchline reframes it: the five stars are
for a nemesis, not a friend. The praise and the insult are the same
sentence, which is what makes it a review rather than just a bark.

**It only works for this dog and this mailman.** Swap in a plumber, a
pizza driver, or any stranger at the door and the joke dies: only the
mailman comes every day, gets barked off the same porch, and delivers
things a dog can lick. No interchangeable pun carries it.

## Alternatives considered

- A one-star rant about eating the mail ("Sir, that is MY mail slot now")
  — funnier as a complaint, but the dog-as-victim angle is weaker than the
  dog-as-victor angle, and it has no turn at the end.
- "The mailman brings me a new chew toy every morning — it's called 'the
  mail'" — a good image, but the punchline lands in the middle and the
  line coasts after it.

The five-star nemesis keeps the joke tight to the end, which is where the
judge's fourth criterion lives.
